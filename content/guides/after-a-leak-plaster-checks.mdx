A brown tide mark on a ceiling is only the part you can see. In Brisbane the leak is often a summer storm, a tired flashing, or a slow pipe, and the plaster behind the paint can be soft, sagging, or perfectly sound. Those are different repairs. Patching the wrong one wastes the paint and, on a claim, can throw away the evidence.

This is the check we walk through before any board comes down.

## Stop the water, then look

Nothing useful happens to the plaster while water is still arriving. Find the source first: roof sheet or tile, valley, gutter overflow, a bathroom waste, an air-conditioning tray, or a pipe in the wall. If you only dry the stain, the next wet week draws a fresh one in the same place.

If water is tracking near a light or a downlight, leave that circuit off and do not open the fitting. Water and a ceiling rose are an electrician's job, not a patching job.

Catch what you can in a bucket, move furniture, and resist the urge to screw the belly of the ceiling back up "just for tonight". A sheet that has let go of its adhesive will not stay put on a couple of screws, and driving them through a wet sheet usually cracks the face you were hoping to save.

## Photograph it before anything is pulled down

Take the photos while the damage is still honest.

- A wide shot of the whole ceiling or wall, so the size is obvious.
- A close-up of the stain, any bubbling paint, and the cornice where it meets the wall.
- The sag, if there is one, photographed from the side so the belly shows against a straight cornice line.
- The roof space or the wet wall cavity, if you can see it safely without standing on the plaster itself.

Note the date. If this might be an insurance job, do not throw the damaged sheets out before the assessor has seen them, and do not repaint the evidence away the same afternoon. Written scopes, photos and a moisture check are what a claim actually needs. The detail of how we document that is on our [insurance plaster repairs](/services/insurance-plaster-repairs) page.

## A stain, a soft sheet, and a sag are three different jobs

They often arrive together after a storm. Treat them separately.

**A stain on a sound sheet.** Press the board. If it is still firm, does not crumble, and the ceiling line is straight, the sheet may be able to stay. The paint will not. A water stain bleeds through ordinary acrylic, including ordinary undercoat. Once the leak is fixed and the sheet is actually dry, the face needs a stain-blocking sealer made for water stains, then paint. Painting the same day seals the mark in.

**A soft, swollen, or mould-marked sheet.** If the paper face has bubbled, the core feels spongy, or there is black mould in the paper, that area wants replacing, not skimming. Compound does not bond to rotten paper, and paint does not kill mould that is still damp inside the sheet. In a bathroom, laundry, or other wet area, the replacement should be water-resistant board. Standard plasterboard that has been soaked does not become the right board by drying out. That is the work covered on [water damage plaster repairs](/services/water-damage-plaster-repairs).

**A sag or a belly.** This is the one people skim and then see again in six months. On a lot of Brisbane ceilings the original sheet was stuck to the battens with daubs of adhesive. Age, roof-space heat, and a leak that adds weight all let that glue go. The board then hangs between the battens. Screwing the belly flat without new adhesive, or coating over the curve, does not put the sheet back on the frame. The repair is to re-bond it, re-screw it at proper centres, and check the plane is flat before any finish goes on. Replace the sheet when it is delaminated, repeatedly soaked, or the battens themselves are damaged. More on that is on [sagging ceiling repairs](/services/sagging-ceiling-repairs).

Look in the roof space if it is safe to do so. A stain on the face with sodden insulation and daylight through the tiles is not a painting problem. Neither is a batten that has been eaten out or split. Lining goes back after the structure and the leak are sorted, not before.

## What not to paint over

Brisbane humidity is patient. A coat that feels dry to the eye can still be cool to the back of your hand, and cool means it is not ready. Thin skim coats can dry in a few hours. Deeper fills, and anything that got properly wet, need longer. Painting onto compound that is still cool traps moisture and the paint fails.

Do not keep filling a crack that has already opened once. A hairline settlement crack in a wall can be a careful DIY job. A crack that comes back is movement in the frame or the truss, and filler alone has no give. It needs tape bedded into the joint so the repair can move with the house. Ceilings, wide cracks, and anything that has been wet are in the "call someone" column. The plain version of that line is on our [DIY plaster repairs](/diy-plaster-repairs) guide.

Do not sand a soft, mouldy, or suspect old sheet to "clean it up" before you know what it is. Sanding spreads the face, and on some older ceilings it is the wrong material entirely.

## Cornice and the cracks that show up later

Water and a bit of frame movement open mitres. A cornice that has let go of the wall, or a mitre that has dropped, is not fixed with a smear of filler or a bead of gap filler along the edge. It needs re-setting, and sometimes a short re-run so the profile matches. Forcing it back while the backing is still wet just pops it off again as things dry. [Cornice repairs](/services/cornice-repairs) covers cracks, matching, and re-runs.

Give the house a chance to dry before you judge the last cracks. A ceiling that took on water can show new hairlines at the cornice and across joints a week later, once the timber has moved. Filling those on day one, then again on day ten, is how a small leak becomes three coats of the wrong product.

## Older fibre-cement ceilings

Not every Brisbane ceiling is plasterboard. Some pre-1990 houses still have fibre-cement sheeting. If the ceiling is hard, dense, and does not look like modern plasterboard, do not cut it and do not sand it. It needs to be identified before anyone puts a hole in it. We stop and flag that on sight, before any cutting.

## A sensible order of work

1. Stop the water and make the area safe. Lights near the leak stay off.
2. Photograph the room, the stain, the cornice, and any sag. Keep the date.
3. Let the framing dry. Do not close a wet cavity, and do not screw a sagging sheet up as a temporary lid.
4. Decide which problem you actually have: a stain on sound board, a sheet that has to come out, or a ceiling that needs re-bonding.
5. Only then tape, coat, seal stains, and paint. Flat and dry first. Paint last.
