A nail pop or a screw pop is the small round bump, or the little crater, where a fastener has moved in a plasterboard wall or ceiling. It is not a crack along a joint, and it is not a picture-hook hole. Filling the paint and leaving the fastener as it is usually brings the same spot back.

This is the difference we look for before anyone smears compound over it.

## What you are looking at

Press nothing hard, and do not poke a ceiling that is also sagging or stained. From the floor, a pop is local. The rest of the sheet is still flat, and the mark is a circle around a nail or screw head, not a line that runs to the cornice.

A hairline that follows a sheet joint, or that opens in the same corner every dry winter, is movement in the frame or the truss. That is a different repair, covered in [cracks in plaster](/guides/plaster-cracks-queensland). Filling either fault with a tube of filler and no other work is how the same patch gets redone next year.

Gyprock, plasterboard and drywall are the same lining. The fastener is what has moved. The paint is only reporting it.

## Why they show up here

Brisbane timber moves. A dry winter and a humid summer change the framing a little, and the board and the fastener do not always move together. The head breaks the compound, and you see a dimple or a bump.

On a lot of 1980s to 2000s ceilings the sheets were stuck to the battens with daubs of adhesive and fixed sparsely. Roof-space heat works on that glue year after year. A single pop can be one fastener that has let go. A field of them, especially with a belly in the sheet, is usually the adhesive letting go between the battens — the same failure as a sag, showing up before the whole sheet drops. Screwing the middle of that belly flat, without new adhesive, cracks the face and does not hold. [Sagging ceiling repairs](/services/sagging-ceiling-repairs) is the page for that pattern.

A pop that arrives with a brown stain, bubbling paint, or straight after a storm is a leak until the source is fixed and the sheet is actually dry. Do not paint it the same day. What to photograph, and what not to close up, is in [after a leak](/guides/after-a-leak-plaster-checks).

## Why filler on its own comes back

The head is still there. If it is proud of the sheet, or the fastener is loose in the timber, compound and paint are sitting on something that will move again. The next dry spell pops the fill straight out.

That is why a careful wall repair resets the fastener first. The nail or screw has to sit just below the face and actually bite solid timber. A spinning head that will not tighten needs a new screw into the stud or batten. Only then is the dimple filled, left to dry, sanded and spot-primed. Compound takes paint differently to the surrounding wall, so an unprimed dimple flashes even when the fill is flat.

If you cannot find solid timber, or the head keeps spinning, stop. More filler will not invent a fixing.

One or two pops on a wall, at eye level, on a smooth surface, can be that careful job. Anything overhead is a different matter. Ceilings show every ridge in raking light, and a ceiling full of pops is not a weekend of tube filler. The line between what is reasonable at eye level and what wants a plasterer is the same one on [DIY plaster repairs](/diy-plaster-repairs).

## When it is more than a pop

Pause the filling if you have any of these:

- Pops in a line along a batten, or scattered across a ceiling that has a belly. The sheet is coming off the frame. Re-bonding and re-screwing a sound sheet, or replacing one that has delaminated, is the repair. Coating the curve is not.
- The paper face is soft, swollen or mould-marked. Compound does not bond to rotten paper. That area wants replacing once the leak is fixed and the framing is dry, which is [water damage plaster repairs](/services/water-damage-plaster-repairs).
- The ceiling is hard, dense, and does not look like modern plasterboard. Some pre-1990 houses still have fibre-cement sheeting. Do not sand it and do not cut it. It needs to be identified before anyone puts a hole in it. We stop and flag that on sight.
- The same spots were filled, painted, and have popped again. The fastener was never reset. Doing the fill a second time, the same way, ends the same way.

A dry ceiling with a few pops is usually a slow problem. A saturated sheet, or a sag you can see from the doorway, is not. Keep people and pets out from under a wet or bulging ceiling. Waterlogged board is heavy and can drop.

## What to photograph

A few photos sort a single pop from a ceiling that needs re-fixing.

- The whole wall or ceiling, so the number of pops is obvious.
- A close-up of one dimple, with the head in the shot if you can see it.
- Any sag, photographed from the side so a belly shows against a straight cornice line.
- A stain, bubbling paint or mould, if it is there. Note the date.

Text them through on 0425 743 992. Small patches are usually quoted from the photos. If the damage is overhead and more than a dimple, [ceiling repairs](/services/ceiling-repairs) is the broader page, and ordinary wall and ceiling patching is [plaster repairs](/services/plaster-repairs).

Painting comes last. Thin fills can dry in a few hours. Deeper ones need longer in Brisbane humidity, and longer again in wet weather. If the compound still feels cool, it is not ready. The finished surface is left paint-ready for your painter. A fresh pop is not something to coat the same afternoon.
