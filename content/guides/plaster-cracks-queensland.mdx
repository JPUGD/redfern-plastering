A crack in a Queensland plaster wall is usually the lining, not the house falling down. Timber frames move a little with the seasons, roof trusses lift in a dry winter, and the paint is the first thing to show it. Filling every line the week it appears is how the same corner gets redone next year.

A few cracks are different. They come back, they open up, or they sit in a ceiling that is starting to sag. Those are worth a look before anyone sands them flat.

## A hairline on a wall

If the crack is narrower than a 20c coin, it is on a wall rather than a ceiling, and it has not grown since you noticed it, it is cosmetic. You see them where sheets meet, beside a door frame after a dry spell, and in paint that has gone brittle.

You can tape and fill that one. Scrape it open slightly, bed paper tape into compound, then coat and sand. Filled with no tape, it splits again as soon as the frame moves. Nail holes and this sort of hairline are the jobs covered on [DIY plaster repairs](/diy-plaster-repairs). Anything wider, anything overhead, or anything that has already been filled once belongs further down this page.

## A crack that comes back

A crack you filled, painted, and watched reopen is movement. The fill has no give, so the next season tears straight through it. In Brisbane that movement is often the roof truss lifting the ceiling edge in dry weather, or the wall frame settling a millimetre. The lining is reporting it. It is not, by itself, a sign the wall is failing.

The repair that holds is the same idea as the hairline, done properly: open the joint, tape it, and feather the coats wide enough that the repair can flex with the house. A bead of gap filler along the paint line will not. That is ordinary [plaster repair](/services/plaster-repairs) work, including the cracks that show up again every winter in hallway ceilings.

Give it a week of ordinary weather before you decide it is back. A line that appears in the first dry week of winter and then sits still is seasonal. A line that keeps getting longer or wider over that week is the next section.

## Ceiling cracks

Ceilings show everything, and they are the wrong place to experiment.

**A fine crack where the ceiling meets the cornice.** Often the same corner, every dry winter. The mitre is the weak point, and truss movement opens it. Filling the paint crack and repainting it usually lasts until the next dry spell. Re-setting the joint is the repair. [Cornice repairs](/services/cornice-repairs) covers cracks, matching and re-runs.

**A crack with a belly in the sheet.** The board has often let go of the adhesive that held it to the battens, and it is hanging between them. Coating the curve does not put it back. Neither does driving a few screws through the middle to flatten it for now. On a sheet that has let go, that usually cracks the face and does not hold. If the sheet is also soft or stained, keep people and pets out from under it. A waterlogged sheet is heavy and can drop. The repair is to re-bond and re-screw a sound sheet, or replace one that is delaminated or soaked. [Sagging ceiling repairs](/services/sagging-ceiling-repairs) and [ceiling repairs](/services/ceiling-repairs) are the two pages for that.

**A crack that arrived with a stain, bubbling paint, or after a storm.** Treat it as a leak until the source is fixed and the sheet is actually dry. Do not paint over it the same day, and do not sand a soft face. If water is tracking near a light or a downlight, leave that circuit off and do not open the fitting. Water and a ceiling rose are an electrician's job. What to photograph, and what not to close up, is on [after a leak](/guides/after-a-leak-plaster-checks). If the paper is swollen or mouldy, that area wants replacing, which is [water damage plaster repairs](/services/water-damage-plaster-repairs).

## When to stop and get the structure checked

Plastering does not fix a house that is moving more than the lining can tolerate. Pause the filling if you have any of these:

- One side of the crack sitting proud of the other, or a gap you can fit a fingertip into, and it is still opening.
- A straight crack that runs the length of a sheet joint or a batten and keeps widening, rather than a hairline that shows each winter and then stops.
- The same crack continuing out of the plaster and into the brickwork outside.
- Doors or windows that suddenly will not close, or a floor that has gone out of level in the same week the crack appeared.

That is a builder's look at the frame or the footings, before anyone makes the lining look finished. We repair the plaster once that is sorted. We do not diagnose structural movement, and a coat of compound will not stop it.

## Older fibre-cement ceilings

Not every Brisbane ceiling is plasterboard. Some pre-1990 houses still have fibre-cement sheeting. If the ceiling is hard, dense, and does not look like modern plasterboard, do not cut the crack open and do not sand it to clean the edges. It needs to be identified before anyone puts a hole in it. We stop and flag that on sight, before any cutting.

## What to photograph

A few photos sort most of these without a visit.

- The whole wall or ceiling, so the length is obvious.
- A close-up with a 20c coin or a ruler in the shot.
- The cornice, and the nearest door frame.
- Any sag, stain, or soft spot, photographed from the side if the sheet has a belly.
- The same crack a week later if you think it is moving. Note the date.
